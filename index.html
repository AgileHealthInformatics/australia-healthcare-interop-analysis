Australian healthcare interoperability capability

A national capability assessment of governance, identity, standards, FHIR implementation, exchange infrastructure and realised workflow interoperability, with jurisdictional analysis and comparison with New Zealand, the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and Ireland.

Scope: national and state/territory capabilityEvidence cut-off: 16 August 2026Method: primary-source weightedOverall assurance: Amber–Green

1. Executive judgement

Overall judgement: Amber–Green. Australia now has one of the more coherent national interoperability architectures among federated health systems. The combination of national healthcare identifiers, AUCDI, AU Core, My Health Record, Share by Default, a formal conformance approach and the developing Health Connect Australia exchange fabric is strategically sound. The principal weakness is not the absence of standards. It is the distance between national architecture and routine clinical workflow.

Fact. The National Healthcare Interoperability Plan 2023–2028 defines 44 actions across identity, standards, information sharing, innovation and benefits measurement. WEB-01 In August 2026, the Australian Digital Health Agency announced that AU Core and AUCDI would become the national baseline for FHIR connections to My Health Record. WEB-03

Analysis. This is more significant than simply adopting FHIR. Australia is constructing a layered national contract: identities identify the participants, AUCDI establishes the clinical content, AU Core establishes reusable FHIR constraints, use-case specifications specialise the baseline, and national services provide discovery and exchange. That architecture is internally coherent and compares favourably with Canada’s emerging CACDI/CA Core+ model and the UK’s more distributed standards landscape.

Fact. The 2025 Interoperability Follow-up Survey provides a strong counterweight to the policy narrative. Digital searching rose to 44% from 31% in 2022, yet sending and receiving still relied on fax, email, post, telephone, in-person exchange and patients themselves. Only 20% of GP clinics used digital systems when sending patient information outwards, and 20% of healthcare professionals reported using printers 20 or more times per day. WEB-02

Central conclusion. Australia’s interoperability problem is no longer principally “which standard should we choose?”. The more important question is whether national standards, conformance and exchange services can displace manual workflow, reduce jurisdictional variation and establish reliable information quality at the point of use.

2. Method and evidence

The assessment gives greatest weight to legislation, government decisions, national strategies, official implementation guides, programme reports and jurisdictional health-service publications. International comparisons use the same principle: Health New Zealand, Manatū Hauora and HL7 New Zealand for New Zealand; ASTP/ONC and CMS for the United States; Canada Health Infoway and Parliament of Canada for Canada; NHS England for UK core standards and operational examples; and Irish legislation, government and HIQA material for Ireland.

The capability ratings are qualitative assurance judgements, not mathematical maturity scores. They are designed to expose where the evidence supports confidence and where architectural intent has not yet become demonstrated operational capability. A Green rating therefore means strong assurance within the assessed dimension, not perfection.

The 2025 Australian interoperability survey is particularly important because it tests realised behaviour rather than policy intent. It surveyed 792 healthcare providers and included 33 further interviews. WEB-02 State and territory analysis is based on public evidence about digital-record integration and interoperability foundations. It is not an official jurisdictional maturity ranking.

Why the layering matters

A FHIR profile is not a national interoperability strategy. Australia’s stronger design choice is to separate the clinical data content from the FHIR representation and then specialise that baseline for use cases. This avoids making one API specification carry policy, semantic, workflow and infrastructure responsibilities simultaneously.

Professional inference. The model remains incomplete if conformance is interpreted only as syntactic or profile compliance. Clinical safety requires traceability from source capture through transformation and exchange to intended use. AU Core’s own future-work list identifies unresolved areas around last-updated information, provenance, missing data and write/update behaviour. WEB-05

5. Legal, regulatory and policy basis

National legal foundation

Australia’s framework combines the My Health Record legislative regime, the Healthcare Identifiers regime, privacy law and more recent reforms. This is important because identity, record access, privacy and sharing obligations are not left solely to voluntary technical standards.

Fact. The 2025 Sharing by Default framework creates legal obligations beginning with pathology and diagnostic imaging. WEB-09 The 2025 identifier reforms broaden the purposes and service capabilities associated with the national identity infrastructure. WEB-08

Standards mandate

Fact. The August 2026 AU Core/AUCDI decision is a national conformance decision for connections to My Health Record. WEB-03

Legal/professional inference. That should not be described as a statutory requirement that every Australian health system use AU Core for every internal or bilateral interface. Its practical reach will expand as national services, procurement requirements and use-case specifications make conformance commercially and operationally necessary.

Assurance point. Australia is increasingly using both standards governance and policy levers. This is more credible than expecting voluntary standards publication alone to overcome vendor incentives, legacy interfaces and fragmented commissioning.

6. Fairness and stakeholder analysis

Interoperability is not distributionally neutral. The 2025 survey shows that the largest operational burden is often experienced at organisational boundaries and in settings with weaker digital capability. Allied health, aged care and cross-provider information exchange remain important pressure points. WEB-02

Consumers

Access improves only if the record is complete

Sharing by Default strengthens consumer and clinician access to key information, but a national record that omits locally held information can still create false confidence. Coverage, currency and provenance therefore matter as much as portal availability.

Providers

Mandate without workflow support can move burden

Providers need usable implementation patterns, conformance tooling, identity integration and workflow redesign. Otherwise standards compliance can create duplicate data-entry or local workarounds rather than interoperability.

Rural, remote and First Nations care

National reach is strategically valuable

A common identity and exchange layer has particular potential where patients move across long distances and organisational boundaries. Good practice requires co-design, culturally appropriate governance and practical support for lower-capability settings rather than assuming uniform readiness.

Analysis. Fair implementation therefore requires differential support while retaining common conformance outcomes. A uniform standard is desirable; a uniform implementation burden is not necessarily fair.

7. Coherence and consistency

Where the model is coherent

The strongest feature is the relationship between national identity, AUCDI, AU Core and specific use cases. This resembles a well-formed standards ecosystem rather than a monolithic canonical model. AUCDI can evolve the shared clinical information requirements, while AU Core can evolve the minimum FHIR representation and search behaviour. Downstream guides can add context without redefining the whole national baseline. WEB-04 WEB-06

Health Connect Australia also fits coherently into that architecture. It is described as a national health information exchange programme, while My Health Record remains a national record infrastructure. WEB-10 The distinction is valuable: a shared record and an exchange fabric solve related but different problems.

Where coherence breaks down operationally

The state and territory landscape remains heterogeneous. ACT and South Australia have strong jurisdiction-wide record foundations; NSW is mid-rollout; Tasmania is earlier in transformation; Victoria remains more decentralised; Western Australia distinguishes a completed DMR layer from its emerging EMR; and the published NT status is insufficiently current for a firm conclusion. This is not evidence that the national strategy is failing. It is evidence that Australia must govern interoperability across different maturity states.

Analysis. The national architecture will be tested most severely at the edges between national services, state platforms, primary care, private specialists, aged care and allied health. Those edges are also where the survey finds continuing manual transfer. WEB-02

8. Clarity and drafting

The National Healthcare Interoperability Plan is comparatively clear about priority areas and actions. WEB-01 The architecture is less clear to outsiders because several overlapping terms are used across the ecosystem: My Health Record, Health Connect Australia, AUCDI, AU Core, AU Base, use-case implementation guides, conformance profiles and jurisdictional digital records.

Clarity strength

Core versus use case

AU Core explicitly supports downstream implementation guides and annual maintenance. This is sound standards engineering because national commonality can be separated from use-case specificity. WEB-05

Clarity risk

“FHIR-compliant” can imply too much

FHIR conformance does not establish clinical completeness, semantic accuracy, provenance, workflow integration or fitness for use. These qualities need explicit assurance rather than being implied by a standards label.

Professional inference. National implementation guidance would benefit from a single public architecture view showing which requirements are normative at each layer, which service or legislation enforces them, and which conformance tests demonstrate compliance. This would reduce the risk of vendors treating AU Core as either optional guidance or a complete end-to-end interoperability specification.

9. Evidence, monitoring and viability

Evidence of progress

The programme has credible governance, regular progress reporting, a dedicated identifiers roadmap, a FHIR accelerator and repeat survey measurement. WEB-01 WEB-07 WEB-11 This is materially better than relying on adoption anecdotes.

Evidence of the remaining problem

The survey finding is unambiguous: digital capability is growing, but the exchange fabric experienced by clinicians remains patchy. Diagnostic imaging digital exchange was reported at 47% in 2025, slightly down from 50% in 2022, while paper and hybrid import remained common. WEB-02

Viability

The national direction is viable because it is evolutionary. It does not require states and providers to abandon every local system. Instead it creates common identity, semantic and API contracts at the exchange boundary. The August 2026 staged transition away from older My Health Record connection patterns also reduces the risk of a single “big bang” cutover. WEB-03

The principal viability risk is that technical conformance may outpace operational adoption. If local systems expose AU Core FHIR while clinicians still print, scan, fax or manually reconcile information, national conformance could improve without delivering proportionate clinical benefit. A second risk is data quality: AU Core’s outstanding provenance, missing-data and write/update questions show that a computable interface is not yet a complete trustworthy-data contract. WEB-05

International comparison

Comparison is made across capability dimensions rather than by assuming that a single “interoperability maturity” score is meaningful. Australia, New Zealand, the USA and Canada each combine national direction with distributed delivery in different ways; the UK consists of four distinct national health systems; Ireland is smaller and more centralised but is earlier in national EHR implementation. New Zealand is especially useful as a comparator because it shares many standards and institutional characteristics with Australia while taking a more explicitly federated information-platform route.

What each comparator reveals

New Zealand

Strong foundations, incomplete binding layer

New Zealand combines national NHI and HPI identity services, NZCDI, NZHTS and a FHIR-first policy. WEB-37 WEB-38 WEB-41 WEB-42 Its current NZ Base IG provides reusable national artefacts but explicitly does not define complete implementation conformance, while Health NZ describes an NZ Core FHIR IG as a future project. WEB-40 Australia is therefore one step ahead in establishing a national minimum FHIR contract.

United States

Strongest enforcement

TEFCA establishes a nationwide trusted-exchange floor, while federal certification, USCDI, standardised APIs, information-blocking policy and CMS payer rules create strong coercive incentives. WEB-22 WEB-23 WEB-24 The counterpoint is identity: the US lacks Australia’s single national healthcare identifier service.

Canada

Closest architectural analogue

CACDI and CA Core+ closely resemble Australia’s AUCDI/AU Core separation, and pan-Canadian governance is explicitly federated. WEB-25 WEB-26 However, the Connected Care for Canadians Act remained at second reading in the House of Commons on 16 August 2026, so the national legal lever is not yet equivalent to an enacted obligation. WEB-27

United Kingdom

Operational depth, structural complexity

The UK has extensive operational FHIR and legacy interoperability, but the four nations are distinct implementation environments. NHS England’s UK Core page explicitly describes the unified FHIR R4 specification as work in progress and not for implementation, even while standards such as BaRS use FHIR UK Core 4 operationally in England. WEB-28 WEB-29

Ireland

Legal acceleration, implementation lag

The Health Information Act 2026 is a substantial change. It provides for electronic health records and permits requests that specify digital format and interoperability requirements. WEB-31 Yet the national EHR procurement only received approval to commence in February 2026 and the national interoperability framework remains in development. WEB-32 WEB-33

Comparative judgement. Australia is not clearly “ahead” of every comparator in a single linear sense. The USA leads on enforceable market-wide interoperability machinery; the UK has deep operational service capability; Canada is converging on a similar national-core model; Ireland now has a much stronger statutory basis. New Zealand is the closest conceptual comparator for national identity and federated information architecture, but its minimum national FHIR conformance layer and shared-record implementation are less mature. Australia’s particular strength is therefore the coherence of its layered national architecture. Its weakness remains the still-visible implementation gap.

11. Recommended actions

Publish one normative national interoperability architecture

Show, in one maintained architecture, the relationship between AUCDI, AU Base, AU Core, use-case guides, identifiers, terminology, conformance, My Health Record and Health Connect Australia. Mark which requirements are mandatory, for whom, under what authority and from what date.

Measure workflow displacement, not only standards adoption

Make reduction in fax, printing, scanning, manual re-entry and patient-mediated transfer explicit national outcome measures. Repeat the provider survey and publish segment-level progress by care setting.

Make data quality a first-order interoperability requirement

Extend conformance beyond structure to include provenance, currency, completeness expectations, mapping assurance and fitness-for-use metadata. AU Core’s own deferred issues provide a practical starting point. WEB-05

Use procurement and funding to drive conformance

Require vendors and publicly funded implementations to demonstrate conformance using reusable test tooling. Avoid creating separate bespoke profiles where an agreed national core and use-case guide can meet the need.

Target the weak edges of the ecosystem

Prioritise primary-to-specialist, aged-care, allied-health, pathology, imaging and cross-jurisdiction workflows where manual transfer remains visible. National success should be judged by difficult cross-boundary journeys, not by interoperability inside a single modern EHR.

Publish state and territory implementation dependencies

Create a transparent national dependency view showing when jurisdictional platforms can consume or expose each national standard. This would distinguish “standard available” from “standard operational at scale”.

Retain the layered model

Do not collapse AUCDI, AU Core and use-case guides into a single canonical specification. The current separation of clinical content, reusable FHIR baseline and workflow-specific requirements is a strength and should be protected.

13. Limitations

Jurisdictional evidence is uneven. State and territory digital-health programmes publish different kinds of information at different frequencies. The map is therefore an analytic view of jurisdictional digital-record integration, not an official interoperability maturity assessment. The Northern Territory is explicitly lower confidence because the principal public Acacia implementation page is not current enough to establish present rollout completion.

International comparability is imperfect. New Zealand is more centralised organisationally than Australia but retains distributed source systems and a staged national information-sharing model. The US and Canadian systems distribute responsibility differently from Australia. “UK” is particularly problematic because England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have distinct health-service architectures. The UK comparison therefore combines UK-wide standards direction with selected England operational evidence and is labelled accordingly.

Implementation outcomes lag policy changes. The August 2026 Australian AU Core/AUCDI baseline decision is extremely recent. Its operational effect cannot yet be demonstrated. New Zealand’s 2026 NZCDI remains in draft consultation and its Shared Digital Health Record is in staged delivery following a July 2026 timetable extension. Likewise, Ireland’s Health Information Act 2026 and national EHR procurement materially improve future capability but should not be mistaken for already realised interoperability.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Where public material did not establish a capability, the report does not infer that the capability does not exist. This applies especially to vendor-specific implementations, local interfaces and unpublished state programme milestones.
